The photon: energy, momentum and its collisions — 7 questions, 10 marks

ISC 2026 · Q1(B)(i)[1]

Calculate the momentum of a photon having an energy of 4·8 × 10−19 J.

A photon is massless, so its momentum comes from E = pc:p = E/c = 4·8 × 10−19 / 3 × 108 = 1·6 × 10−27 kg m s−1
NoteDo not try p = mv. A photon has zero rest mass; its momentum is E/c = h/λ.
ISC Specimen 2027 · Q1(B)(iv)[1]

What is the difference between a moving metallic ball colliding with another ball in motion, and a photon colliding with an electron?

CISCE’s printed answer
When a photon collides with an electron it gives up either its whole energy or none at all — the interaction is all-or-nothing, one photon to one electron. A moving ball can transfer any fraction of its energy in a collision.
ISC Specimen 2027 · Q1(B)(v)[1]

When can a gamma ray photon produce a proton and an antiproton pair?

CISCE’s printed answer
Only when the photon carries at least the rest-mass energy of both particles:Emin = 2mpc2 = 938·3 + 938·3 = 1876·6 MeV
NotePair production also needs a nearby nucleus to take up momentum; a photon cannot do it in empty space and still conserve both energy and momentum.
ISC Specimen 2026 · Q1(B)(vi)[1]

What happens when an electron collides with a positron?

CISCE’s printed answer
They annihilate each other, the whole of their mass appearing as energy in the form of gamma-ray photons.
CISCE Competency booklet · Q60[2]

In positron emission tomography (PET) an emitted positron meets an electron, the two annihilate, and two γ-rays of 0·511 MeV each move directly away from one another. (a) Why are two γ photons produced? (b) Why is the energy of each 0·511 MeV?

CISCE’s printed answer
(a) To conserve momentum. The electron and positron are very nearly at rest, so the total momentum before is almost zero; a single photon would carry momentum away, so two must be emitted in opposite directions.
(b) From E = mc2, the energy equivalent of one electron mass is 9·1 × 10−31 × (3 × 108)2 ≈ 8·2 × 10−14 J = 0·511 MeV. The two rest masses share out as one such photon each.
CISCE Competency booklet · Q50[3]

A charged particle is passed through crossed electric and magnetic fields. Under what circumstance does it suffer no deviation? Also obtain a relation showing that electromagnetic waves carry both energy and momentum.

CISCE’s printed answer
The particle goes straight through when the electric and magnetic forces balance:qE = qvB  ⇒  v = E/BAn electromagnetic wave delivering energy U to a surface delivers momentump = U/cso the wave carries both. It is this momentum that produces radiation pressure.
NoteThe booklet files this under Dual Nature, though it is really an electromagnetic-waves question. CISCE's printed key loses the numerator and shows only “p = /c”; the quantity on top is the energy U.
NCERT Exemplar Chapter 11 · 11.2[1]

The wavelength of a photon needed to remove a proton from a nucleus to which it is bound with 1 MeV of energy is nearly (a) 1·2 nm (b) 1·2 × 10−3 nm (c) 1·2 × 10−6 nm (d) 1·2 × 101 nm

the NCERT Exemplar’s printed answer
(b) 1·2 × 10−3 nm. Using hc = 1240 eV nm,λ = hc/E = 1240 / 106 = 1·24 × 10−3 nm— about 1 pm, which is a gamma ray.
NoteA binding energy of 1 MeV is a nuclear energy, a million times an atomic one, so the wavelength comes out a million times shorter than visible light. Order of magnitude alone picks the answer out.

Photoelectric effect: the terms and the laws — 10 questions, 17 marks

ISC 2018 · Q9(ii)[1]

With reference to the photoelectric effect, define threshold wavelength.

The largest wavelength of incident radiation that can just eject photoelectrons from a given metal surface. λ0 = hc/φ.
NoteThreshold frequency is a minimum; threshold wavelength is a maximum. They are the same condition written two ways.
ISC 2019 · Q10[2]

Define, with reference to the photoelectric effect: (a) threshold frequency (ν0) (b) stopping potential (Vs).

(a) The minimum frequency of incident radiation below which no photoelectrons are emitted from a given metal, however intense the radiation.
(b) The minimum negative potential applied to the collector at which the photocurrent becomes zero; it measures the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons, eVs = Kmax.
ISC 2025 · Q8[2]

With reference to the photoelectric effect, define (i) threshold frequency (ii) work function.

(i) The minimum frequency of the incident radiation that can just cause photoelectric emission from a given metal.
(ii) The minimum energy required to free an electron from the surface of the metal, φ = hν0.
ISC 2018 · Q1(iv)[1]

Einstein's photoelectric equation is: (a) Emax = hλ − φ (b) Emax = hν − φ (c) Emax = hν + φ (d) Emax = hλ + φ

(b) Emax = hν − φ. The photon brings hν; φ is spent freeing the electron and the rest appears as kinetic energy.
NoteIt is hν, never hλ — energy rises with frequency and falls with wavelength.
ISC 2024 · Q16(i)[1]

In an experiment on the photoelectric effect, how does the stopping potential change, if at all, when the intensity of the incident monochromatic UV radiation is increased?

It does not change. The stopping potential depends only on the frequency of the radiation and the work function; raising the intensity sends more photons and so a larger current, but each photon still carries the same energy hν.
ISC 2025 Improvement · Q8[2]

The intensity of monochromatic UV light incident on the photocathode of a photoelectric cell is increased. What is the effect on (i) the photocurrent (ii) the kinetic energy of the most energetic photoelectron?

(i) The photocurrent increases, in direct proportion to the intensity — more photons per second means more electrons per second.
(ii) No change. Kmax = hν − φ involves the frequency only.
ISC Specimen 2025 · Q8[2]

How does the stopping potential in photoelectric emission vary if (i) the intensity of the incident radiation increases (ii) the frequency of the incident radiation decreases?

CISCE’s printed answer
(i) The stopping potential is independent of the intensity of the incident radiation.
(ii) The stopping potential decreases as the frequency decreases (it changes with frequency).
ISC Specimen 2024 · Q14[3]

A certain metal emits photoelectrons with green light but not with yellow light. If the surface is illuminated with blue light and with red light, which of the two will cause photoelectric emission? Give a reason. What conclusion can be drawn from this about the nature of light?

Blue light. In the visible spectrum the order of frequency is red < yellow < green < blue. Since green works and yellow does not, the threshold frequency lies between them. Blue has a higher frequency than green, so it will cause emission; red has a lower frequency than yellow and will not.
The conclusion is that emission depends on the frequency and not the intensity, so light must be delivering its energy in discrete quanta (photons) — light behaves as a particle here.
CISCE Competency booklet · Q12[1]

Ultraviolet light of the same frequency and the same intensity falls on zinc and on sodium. Which of the following may be the same for the two metals? (a) kinetic energy of the photoelectrons (b) photoelectric current (c) stopping potential (d) potential energy of the photoelectrons

CISCE’s printed answer
(b) the photoelectric current. The two metals have different work functions, so Kmax and hence the stopping potential must differ. The current depends on the number of photons arriving per second, which is fixed by the intensity — the same for both.
ISC 2024 · Q16(ii)[2]

Ultraviolet light is incident on metals P, Q and R of work functions 8 eV, 2 eV and 4 eV. (a) Which metal has the lowest threshold frequency? (b) For which metal is Emax the minimum?

(a) Q. ν0 = φ/h, so the smallest work function gives the smallest threshold frequency.
(b) P. Emax = hν − φ, and with the same ν for all three, the largest φ leaves the least kinetic energy.
reference

How is the stopping potential measured?

The collector is made negative with respect to the emitter and the retarding potential is increased until the microammeter reads zero. That potential is Vs, and eVs = Kmax.
NoteIncluded for the diagram, which several of the questions above assume. It is not itself an examination question.

Einstein's photoelectric equation: numericals — 7 questions, 18 marks

ISC 2023 · Q15[3]

Monochromatic light of wavelength 396 nm is incident on a metal of work function 1·125 eV. Calculate (i) the energy of an incident photon in eV (ii) the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons in eV.

Using hc = 1240 eV nm,(i) E = hc/λ = 1240/396 = 3·13 eV(ii) Kmax = E − φ = 3·13 − 1·125 = 2·0 eV
NoteCarrying hc = 1240 eV nm turns this into one division. In SI it is 6·63 × 10−34 × 3 × 108 / 396 × 10−9 = 5·02 × 10−19 J, then divide by 1·6 × 10−19.
ISC Specimen 2022 Sem 2 · Q13(i)[3]

Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons emitted by a metal of work function 1·5 eV when it is illuminated with light of wavelength 198 nm.

E = 1240/198 = 6·26 eVKmax = 6·26 − 1·5 = 4·76 eV ≈ 7·6 × 10−19 J
ISC 2026 · Q8[2]

The work functions of metals M1 and M2 are 1·9 eV and 5·0 eV. Perform the necessary calculation to find which metal emits photoelectrons when monochromatic light of wavelength 410 nm falls on them.

E = hc/λ = 1240/410 = 3·02 eV3·02 eV > 1·9 eV but 3·02 eV < 5·0 eV, so only M1 emits photoelectrons. For M2 the photon has less than the work function and no electron is freed, however bright the light.
ISC Specimen 2026 · Q8[2]

In the photoelectric effect the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectron is a and the work function of the metal is W0. If the frequency of the incident radiation is made K times, calculate the change in the maximum kinetic energy.

CISCE’s printed answer
a1 = hν − W0    a2 = h(Kν) − W0a2 − a1 = Khν − hν = (K − 1)hν
NoteThe work function cancels, so the change does not depend on the metal at all.
ISC Specimen 2026 · Q17[3]

In a hydrogen atom an electron jumps from the first excited state to the ground state and a photon is emitted. This photon falls on a metal surface of work function 2 eV. Calculate the stopping potential of the emitted electron.

CISCE’s printed answer
E = −3·4 − (−13·6) = 10·2 eVeV0 = E − W0 = 10·2 − 2 = 8·2 eVV0 = 8·2 V
NoteTwo chapters in one question. The first excited state is n = 2 at −3·4 eV, not n = 1.
ISC Specimen 2027 · Q16[3]

Three surfaces, all of work function 2·3 eV, are shown illuminated. (i) Why are no photoelectrons emitted in image A? (ii) Which image corresponds to the highest stopping potential? (iii) If the photon energy in image B is 2·8 eV, calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectrons.

CISCE’s printed answer
(i) Because the energy of the incident photons is less than the work function. For emission the photon energy must be at least φ.
(ii) Image C — the highest photon energy gives the largest Kmax and hence the largest stopping potential.
(iii)Kmax = E − φ = 2·8 − 2·3 = 0·5 eV
NCERT Chapter 11 · Exercise 11.6[2]

The threshold frequency for a certain metal is 3·3 × 1014 Hz. If light of frequency 8·2 × 1014 Hz is incident on the metal, predict the cut-off voltage for the photoelectric emission.

NCERT’s printed answer
eV0 = h(ν − ν0)= 6·63 × 10−34 × (8·2 − 3·3) × 1014 = 3·25 × 10−19 JV0 = 3·25 × 10−19 / 1·6 × 10−19 = 2·0 V
NoteSubtract the frequencies first and multiply by h once. Working out hν and φ separately and then subtracting gets there too, but with two chances to lose a power of ten.

Stopping potential against frequency — 8 questions, 19 marks

ISC 2020 · Q1(iv)[1]

In a photoelectric effect experiment, the slope of the graph of stopping potential against frequency gives the value of: (a) h/e (b) h (c) e (d) e/h

(a) h/e. From eVs = hν − φ, Vs = (h/e)ν − φ/e, which is a straight line of slope h/e.
ISC 2025 · Q17[3]

(i) Plot a labelled graph of stopping potential (Vs) against the frequency of the incident UV radiation. (ii) State how the value of Planck's constant can be determined from this graph.

(i) A straight line cutting the frequency axis at ν0 and the Vs axis at −φ/e, as drawn.
(ii) Measure the slope of the line. Since Vs = (h/e)ν − φ/e, the slope is h/e, soh = e × slope
ISC Specimen 2023 · Q15[3]

With reference to the photoelectric effect, plot a labelled graph of stopping potential against the frequency of the incident radiation, and state how you would use it to find Planck's constant.

The same graph and the same method as above: the line is straight, its slope is h/e, and h = e × slope. The intercept on the frequency axis gives ν0 and hence φ = hν0.
ISC 2025 Improvement · Q17[3]

The graph of stopping potential (V0) against the frequency (f) of the incident UV radiation is shown. (i) Find the work function of the metal. (ii) Determine the value of Planck's constant.

(i) The line meets V0 = 0 at the threshold, f0 = 10 × 1014 Hz. Extrapolated back it cuts the V0 axis at about −4·1 V, and that intercept is −φ/e:φ ≈ 4·1 eV = 6·6 × 10−19 J(ii) The slope is 4·1 V / (10 × 1014 Hz) = 4·1 × 10−15 V s, soh = e × slope = 1·6 × 10−19 × 4·1 × 10−15 ≈ 6·6 × 10−34 J s
NoteThis paper is a scan, and the printed graph has to be read by eye, so an examiner will allow a band of answers. The two readings must be consistent: whatever intercept you read, dividing it by f0 and multiplying by e must give something near 6·6 × 10−34 J s.
ISC 2026 · Q17(i)[3]

The graphs show the variation of stopping potential with frequency of the incident radiation for metals A and B, with thresholds f0A and f0B where f0B < f0A. UV radiation of appropriate wavelength falls on both. Which metal emits photoelectrons with the higher Emax? Give a reason.

Metal B. Its threshold frequency is lower, so its work function φ = hf0 is smaller. With the same incident frequency, Emax = hf − φ is therefore larger for B.
NoteThe two lines are parallel — the slope h/e is the same for every metal. Only the intercepts differ.
ISC Specimen 2025 · Q17[3]

The graphs show Vs against ν for two photosensitive materials M1 and M2. Express the work functions in terms of h, the threshold frequency and e, and show that the slope equals (V1 − V2)/(ν01 − ν02) at a frequency above both thresholds.

CISCE’s printed answer
Work functions: W01 = hν01    W02 = hν02At one frequency ν above both thresholds,V1 = (h/e)ν − (h/e)ν01    V2 = (h/e)ν − (h/e)ν02Subtracting, the (h/e)ν terms cancel:V1 − V2 = (h/e)(ν02 − ν01)h/e = (V1 − V2)/(ν01 − ν02)
NoteCISCE's key writes the work functions as hν0/e, which is the work function in volts. In joules it is hν0.
CISCE Competency booklet · Q21[1]

The work function of caesium is 2·04 eV and a graph of photoelectric current against collector plate potential is given. If the frequency of the incident radiation is doubled, which combination is correct? (a) 2·04 eV, −0·6 V (b) 4·08 eV, −0·6 V (c) 4·08 eV, −3·24 V (d) 2·04 eV, −3·24 V

CISCE’s printed answer
(d) work function 2·04 eV, stopping potential −3·24 V. The work function is a property of the metal and cannot change when the light changes. From the graph the original stopping potential is 0·6 V, so hν = φ + eVs = 2·04 + 0·6 = 2·64 eV. Doubling the frequency doubles the photon energy to 5·28 eV, soeVs′ = 5·28 − 2·04 = 3·24 eV
NoteThe trap is option (c): doubling the frequency does not double the work function.
NCERT Chapter 11 · Exercise 11.5[2]

In an experiment on the photoelectric effect, the slope of the cut-off voltage against frequency of incident light is found to be 4·12 × 10−15 V s. Calculate the value of Planck's constant.

NCERT’s printed answer
The slope of that line is h/e, soh = e × slope= 1·6 × 10−19 × 4·12 × 10−15h = 6·59 × 10−34 J s
NoteThis is Millikan's method of measuring h, and it is the reason the graph is worth plotting at all: the slope gives a fundamental constant without needing to know the work function.

de Broglie waves and the wave nature of matter — 16 questions, 20 marks

ISC 2018 · Q9(i)[1]

State de Broglie's hypothesis.

Every moving material particle has a wave associated with it, of wavelengthλ = h/p = h/mv— that is, matter, like radiation, has a dual wave-particle character.
ISC Specimen 2018 · Q1(iv)[1]

The de Broglie wavelength of a moving particle is λ. Its momentum is given by:

p = h/λ, a direct rearrangement of λ = h/p.
NoteThe printed options are images and do not survive text extraction from this paper; the relation itself is what the question tests.
ISC Specimen 2024 · Q1(vi)[1]

The de Broglie wavelength of a moving particle varies: (a) directly with its velocity (b) directly with its momentum (c) directly with its kinetic energy (d) inversely with its linear momentum

(d) inversely with its linear momentum, since λ = h/p.
ISC 2025 · Q1(iii)[1]

If the kinetic energy of moving electrons is made four times, their de Broglie wavelength becomes: (a) eight times (b) four times (c) two times (d) half

(d) half. λ = h/√(2mK), so λ ∝ 1/√K. Four times the kinetic energy halves the wavelength.
ISC 2025 Improvement · Q1(iv)[1]

The de Broglie wavelength of moving particles does not depend on their: (a) velocity (b) mass (c) size (d) kinetic energy

(c) size. λ = h/mv involves the mass and the velocity (and so the kinetic energy), but nothing about the particle's physical dimensions.
ISC 2026 · Q1(iii)[1]

An electron, a proton, a deuteron and an alpha particle are all accelerated from rest through the same potential difference. Which has the maximum de Broglie wavelength?

The electron. For a charge q accelerated through V,λ = h/√(2mqV)so the largest λ goes with the smallest product mq. The electron has both the smallest mass and the smallest charge, so its wavelength is by far the greatest.
ISC Specimen 2022 Sem 2 · Q1(ii)[1]

Find the de Broglie wavelength of electrons moving with a speed of 7 × 106 m s−1.

λ = h/mv = 6·63 × 10−34 / (9·1 × 10−31 × 7 × 106)λ = 6·63 × 10−34 / 6·37 × 10−24 = 1·04 × 10−10 m ≈ 1 Å
NoteAbout one ångström — comparable with atomic spacings in a crystal, which is exactly why electrons can be diffracted by one.
ISC Specimen 2025 · Q1(iv)[1]

The de Broglie wavelength of an electron in the first Bohr orbit of a hydrogen atom is equal to: (a) the diameter of the first orbit (b) the circumference of the first orbit (c) the radius of the second orbit (d) h/2π

CISCE’s printed answer
(b) the circumference of the first orbit. Bohr's quantisation is 2πr = nλ, and for the first orbit n = 1, so λ = 2πr.
ISC Specimen 2026 · Q1(A)(iv)[1]

The wavelength λe of an electron and λp of a photon of the same energy E are related by:

CISCE’s printed answer
λp ∝ λe2. For the photon E = hc/λp, so λp = hc/E. For the electron E = p2/2m = h2/(2mλe2). Substituting,λp = (2mc/h) λe2
NoteThe electron's energy goes as 1/λ2 while the photon's goes as 1/λ. That difference is the whole question.
ISC Specimen 2027 · Q8[2]

Two particles A and B of masses mA and mB have de Broglie wavelengths λA and λB. If λA = 2λB, compare their kinetic energies.

CISCE’s printed answer
K = p2/2m = h2/(2mλ2)KA/KB = (mBλB2)/(mAλA2) = mB/(4mA)KA : KB = mB : 4mA
ISC Specimen 2027 · Q1(A)(iv)[1]

Match the scientist with the contribution: 1 Planck, 2 Einstein, 3 Bohr, 4 de Broglie — A quantisation of angular momentum, B wave nature of particles, C quantum theory, D mass–energy relation.

CISCE’s printed answer
1–C, 2–D, 3–A, 4–B. Planck introduced the quantum theory, Einstein the mass–energy relation, Bohr the quantisation of angular momentum and de Broglie the wave nature of particles.
CISCE Competency booklet · Q39[2]

An electron is revolving around the nucleus of a hydrogen atom. If the radius of the first orbit is 0·53 × 10−10 m, calculate the de Broglie wavelength of the moving electron in the orbit.

CISCE’s printed answer
By Bohr's quantisation condition 2πr = nλ, and n = 1 for the first orbit:λ = 2πr = 2 × 3·14 × 0·53 × 10−10 = 3·33 × 10−10 m
CISCE Competency booklet · Q41[2]

Calculate the wavelength of a photon needed to ionise a hydrogen atom.

CISCE’s printed answer
Ionisation from the ground state needs 13·6 eV:λ = hc/E = (6·6 × 10−34 × 3 × 108) / (13·6 × 1·6 × 10−19)λ = 9·1 × 10−8 m = 910 Å ≈ 91 nm
NoteThis is in the far ultraviolet, which is why visible light cannot ionise hydrogen from the ground state.
ISC 2020 · Q9(ii)[1]

What conclusion can be drawn from the Davisson and Germer experiment?

That electrons behave as waves. The scattered electrons showed a sharp diffraction maximum at a definite angle, and the wavelength deduced from it agreed with λ = h/p — the first direct confirmation of de Broglie's hypothesis.
NCERT Chapter 11 · Exercise 11.10[2]

What is the de Broglie wavelength of a bullet of mass 0·040 kg travelling at 1·0 km s−1?

NCERT’s printed answer
λ = h/mv = 6·63 × 10−34 / (0·040 × 1000)λ = 1·7 × 10−35 m
NoteTwenty-five powers of ten smaller than a nucleus, and so utterly unobservable. That is the answer to “why do we not see the wave nature of ordinary objects?” — h is tiny and everyday momenta are enormous by comparison.
NCERT Exemplar Chapter 11 · 11.6[1]

An electron moving with initial velocity v = v0î enters a magnetic field B = B0ĵ. Its de Broglie wavelength (a) remains constant (b) increases with time (c) decreases with time (d) increases and decreases periodically.

the NCERT Exemplar’s printed answer
(a) remains constant. The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work: the speed, and with it the momentum mv, cannot change. Since λ = h/mv, the wavelength is unchanged — only its direction of travel turns.
NoteContrast the companion Exemplar question with an electric field: there the force does work, the speed changes and λ changes with it. Magnetic field → λ fixed; electric field → λ varies.
