1. Electrostatics

C1Concept
Coulomb's law, and why superposition is the whole of it

Two point charges attract or repel along the line joining them with

F = 14πε0q1q2r2,   14πε0 = 9 × 109 N m2 C−2

With more than two charges there is no new law: the force on any one charge is the vector sum of the forces the others would exert separately, each unaffected by the presence of the rest. Nearly every electrostatics problem is this one sentence plus some geometry.

Watch outThe r in the formula is the distance between the charges, not a coordinate. Squaring the wrong length is the commonest slip.
Hold on to thisSuperpose vectors, never magnitudes — resolve first, and look for symmetry that kills a component before you compute anything.

1. Electrostatics

C2Concept
The electric field, and what its lines tell you

E = F/q0 is the force per unit positive test charge. For a point charge E = kq/r2, directed away from a positive charge and towards a negative one.

Field lines are a picture of that vector field, and they obey rules worth memorising:

  • they begin on positive charge and end on negative charge, or run to infinity — they never begin or end in empty space;
  • they never cross, since the field has one direction at each point;
  • they are closer together where the field is stronger;
  • they meet a conductor's surface at right angles.
Hold on to thisCrowding means strength. That single reading answers a whole family of one-mark questions.

1. Electrostatics

C3Concept
Electric flux

Flux measures how much field crosses a surface:

Δφ = E·ΔS = E ΔS cos θ

where θ is the angle between the field and the normal to the surface — not the surface itself. Flux is a scalar; its SI unit is N m2 C−1.

Two consequences you will use constantly: a surface parallel to the field carries no flux, and a closed surface enclosing no net charge has zero net flux even though the field on it is not zero.

Hold on to thisFlux needs the angle to the normal. When a Gaussian surface is well chosen, cos θ is 1 on the part that matters and 0 everywhere else.

1. Electrostatics

C4Concept
Gauss' theorem

The net flux out of any closed surface depends only on the charge enclosed:

∮ E·dS = qenclosedε0

It is always true, but it is only useful where symmetry lets you take E outside the integral. That needs a surface on which E has one magnitude and a fixed angle to the normal — which is why the three standard applications use a cylinder, a cylinder and a sphere.

Line charge
coaxial cylinder
Plane sheet
cylinder piercing the sheet
Spherical shell
concentric sphere
Hold on to thisGauss' theorem is useless without a symmetry argument. State the symmetry and the choice of surface before you write the integral — the marks are there.

1. Electrostatics

D1Derivation
Gauss' theorem applied to an infinite line of charge

Take a coaxial cylinder of radius r and length l. By symmetry E is radial and constant over the curved surface; through the two flat ends E lies in the surface, so they carry no flux.

E·2πrl = λlε0

The length cancels — it had to, since an infinite line has no special place along it.

ResultE = λ2πε0r, so E ∝ 1/r — not 1/r2.

1. Electrostatics

D2Derivation
Gauss' theorem applied to a plane sheet, and to a conductor

Take a cylinder piercing the sheet symmetrically, of cross-section A. The curved surface carries no flux; the two flat faces each carry EA.

2EA = σAε0

For a charged conductor the field exists on one side only — inside the metal it is zero — so only one face contributes and the answer is twice as large.

ResultSheet: E = σ2ε0, independent of distance. Conductor surface: E = σε0.

1. Electrostatics

D3Derivation
Gauss' theorem applied to a spherical shell

Take a concentric sphere of radius r. By symmetry E is radial and constant on it, so ∮E·dS = E·4πr2.

For r > R the enclosed charge is the whole of q; for r < R it is zero, because all the charge sits on the surface.

ResultOutside: E = kq/r2 — the shell acts as if all its charge were at the centre. Inside: E = 0.

1. Electrostatics

C5Concept
The conductor: field zero inside, normal outside

Free charges rearrange until they feel no force, so in electrostatic equilibrium:

  • E = 0 everywhere inside the metal;
  • the whole conductor is at one potential;
  • all excess charge lies on the outer surface;
  • just outside, E = σ/ε0 and is normal to the surface;
  • charge density, and so the field, is greatest where the surface is most sharply curved.

A hollow conductor therefore screens its cavity completely — a Faraday cage. Magnetic screening can never be as good, because lines of B cannot end on anything.

Hold on to thisInside a conductor: no field, no potential difference, no charge. Outside: the field is perpendicular to the surface.

1. Electrostatics

C6Concept
The electric dipole

Two equal and opposite charges a distance 2l apart have dipole moment p = q(2l), directed from −q to +q; the unit is C m.

On the axis
E = 2kpr3, parallel to p
On the equator
E = kpr3, antiparallel to p

Both fall as 1/r3, faster than a point charge, because the two charges nearly cancel at a distance. The axial field is exactly twice the equatorial one.

Hold on to this1/r3, and axial = 2 × equatorial. If a question gives you a net force on a dipole, the field must be non-uniform.

1. Electrostatics

D4Derivation
Torque and potential energy of a dipole in a uniform field

The two forces qE are equal, opposite and not collinear, so they form a couple with arm 2l sin θ:

τ = qE·2l sin θ = pE sin θ   or   τ = p × E

Turning it through dθ costs pE sin θ dθ. Integrating from the reference position θ = 90°, where U is taken as zero:

U = −pE cos θ = −p·E
ResultStable at θ = 0 (U = −pE), unstable at 180°. Net force is zero in a uniform field — only a couple.

1. Electrostatics

C7Concept
Potential, and its relation to the field

V is the work done per unit charge in bringing a test charge from infinity, and it is a scalar — which is why potentials are far easier to add than fields.

Point charge
V = kq/r
Many charges
V = σkqi/ri, an ordinary sum
Field from potential
E = −dV/dr
Work done
W = q(VB − VA), independent of the path

The minus sign says the field points down the potential hill. A positive charge released from rest moves towards lower potential.

Hold on to thisAdd potentials as numbers, fields as vectors. And V can be zero where E is not, and vice versa.

1. Electrostatics

C8Concept
Equipotential surfaces

A surface on which V is constant. No work is done moving a charge along it, so the field must be perpendicular to it everywhere.

  • For a point charge they are concentric spheres; for a uniform field, parallel planes.
  • Since E = −dV/dr, a fixed step in V needs a small step in r where E is large — so the surfaces crowd where the field is strong.
  • They are equally spaced only in a uniform field.
  • They never intersect.
Hold on to thisEquipotentials ⊥ field lines, and crowded where the field is strong — near a sharp point most of all.

1. Electrostatics

C9Concept
Potential energy of a system of charges

Assemble the charges one at a time and add the work each one costs:

U = 14πε0 σpairs qiqjrij

Count each pair once — three charges give three terms, four give six. The sign follows from the charges: unlike pairs contribute negative energy (they are bound), like pairs positive.

Hold on to thisOne term per pair, with signs. U = 0 does not mean the charges feel no force — it means the assembly cost no net work.

1. Electrostatics

C10Concept
Capacitance, and the parallel plate capacitor

C = Q/V, the charge stored per volt; the unit is the farad, and practical values are pF to µF. For parallel plates of area A a distance d apart:

C = ε0Ad  (air)    C = Kε0Ad  (filled)

Capacitance is a matter of geometry and medium only: it does not depend on the charge or the voltage you happen to apply.

Hold on to thisC ∝ A, ∝ 1/d, ∝ K. Ask what is held constant — charge, if the capacitor is isolated; voltage, if it stays connected to a battery.

1. Electrostatics

C11Concept
Capacitors in series and in parallel
Series
the same charge on each; 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …
Parallel
the same voltage across each; C = C1 + C2 + …

The rules are the opposite way round to resistors, and for the same reason: adding capacitors in parallel is like widening the plates, while adding them in series is like moving the plates apart.

In series the smallest capacitor dominates the combination and takes the largest share of the voltage.

Hold on to thisSeries → same Q. Parallel → same V. Decide which before writing anything down.

1. Electrostatics

C12Concept
Dielectrics and energy stored

A dielectric polarises in the field, and the induced surface charge opposes the applied field, reducing it to E0/K. The capacitance rises by the same factor K.

U = 12CV2 = 12QV = Q22C

Which form to use depends on what is fixed. Insert a slab into an isolated charged capacitor (Q fixed) and the energy falls; do it with the battery still connected (V fixed) and the energy rises, the battery supplying the difference.

Hold on to thisIsolated means Q is fixed; connected means V is fixed. Almost every dielectric question turns on that distinction.

2. Current Electricity

C1Concept
Current, drift velocity and the microscopic picture

A steady current is a slow drift superposed on violent random motion. Between collisions an electron is accelerated by the field; the average velocity it keeps is

vd = eEτm,    I = nAevd

where τ is the relaxation time. The drift speed is astonishingly small — a fraction of a millimetre per second — yet a lamp lights at once, because the field is established through the circuit at nearly the speed of light and every electron starts moving together.

Mobility
µ = vd/E, unit m2 V−1 s−1
Current density
J = I/A = nevd
Hold on to thisI = nAevd. Whenever a question mentions free electrons per cubic metre, this is the equation it wants.

2. Current Electricity

D1Derivation
Ohm's law from the drift picture, and ρ = m/ne2τ

Put the drift velocity into the expression for the current:

I = nAe eEτm  ⇒  J = ne2τmE

which is J = σE — Ohm's law in microscopic form. Writing E = V/l and I = JA gives V = IR with R = ρl/A.

Resultρ = m/ne2τ. Ohm's law holds because τ barely depends on the applied field, not because it is a law of nature.

2. Current Electricity

C2Concept
Resistivity, and how it changes with temperature

R = ρl/A: the resistance belongs to the specimen, the resistivity to the material.

Metal
n is fixed; heating shortens τ, so ρ rises — roughly linearly, ρ = ρ0(1 + αΔT)
Semiconductor
heating frees many more carriers, so n rises far faster than τ falls and ρ drops sharply
Alloys
manganin and constantan have a very small α, which is why standard resistors are made of them
Watch outStretching a wire keeps its volume constant. Double the length and the area halves, so R ∝ l2 — the resistance goes up four times, not two.
Hold on to thisMetals up, semiconductors down — and the reason is which of n and τ wins in ρ = m/ne2τ.

2. Current Electricity

C3Concept
Resistors, cells and internal resistance
Series
same current; R = R1 + R2 + …
Parallel
same voltage; 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + …

A real cell has internal resistance r, so the voltage at its terminals falls as soon as it delivers current:

V = ε − Ir,    I = εR + r

On open circuit I = 0 and V = ε; on short circuit R = 0 and I = ε/r, the largest current the cell can give, with V = 0.

n cells in series
I = nε/(R + nr) — use when R >> r
n in parallel
I = ε/(R + r/n) — use when R << r
Hold on to thisemf is what a cell has; terminal p.d. is what it gives. They are equal only when no current flows.

2. Current Electricity

C4Concept
Kirchhoff's two laws
Junction rule
ΣI = 0 at any junction — charge is conserved
Loop rule
ΣV = 0 round any closed loop — energy is conserved

The whole difficulty is bookkeeping. Fix a direction for each unknown current and keep it; if the answer comes out negative, the current simply runs the other way. Going round a loop, a resistor contributes −IR if you travel with the current and +IR against it; a cell contributes +ε if you enter at the − terminal.

Two unknowns need two independent loops. A third loop tells you nothing new.

Hold on to thisChoose directions, keep them, and let the algebra sort out the signs.

2. Current Electricity

D2Derivation
The balance condition of a Wheatstone bridge

At balance the galvanometer reads zero, so no current flows through BD and B and D are at the same potential. Then I1 passes through P and Q, and I2 through R and S.

Applying the loop rule to the two halves:

I1P = I2R   and   I1Q = I2S

Dividing one by the other eliminates both currents.

ResultP/Q = R/S. At balance the bridge is independent of the cell — which is why it is accurate.

2. Current Electricity

C5Concept
The metre bridge

A Wheatstone bridge in which two arms are the two parts of a uniform wire, so their resistances are in the ratio of their lengths:

RS = l100 − l
  • Keep the null point near the middle — the percentage error in l and (100−l) is then least.
  • Press the jockey gently and vertically, or you change the wire's cross-section where you press.
  • End corrections come from the soldered joins, and are removed by interchanging R and S and averaging.
Hold on to thisBalance ratio = length ratio. Aim for the middle of the wire.

2. Current Electricity

C6Concept
The potentiometer, and why it beats a voltmeter

A steady current in a uniform wire gives a uniform potential gradient k = V/L volts per metre. Any emf is then measured by the length of wire that balances it:

ε = kl

At balance no current is drawn from the cell, so there is no drop across its internal resistance and the true emf is measured. A voltmeter always draws some current and reads the terminal p.d. instead — which is why the potentiometer is called an ideal voltmeter.

Comparing emfs
ε1/ε2 = l1/l2
Internal resistance
r = R(l1/l2 − 1), with the shunt R across the cell
Watch outThe driver cell must have a larger emf than anything you measure, and like terminals must be joined at A, or no balance point exists on the wire.
Hold on to thisNo current at balance ⇒ true emf. l1 is always the open-circuit (longer) length.

2. Current Electricity

C7Concept
Power, heating and transmission
P = VI = I2R = V2/R

Which form to use depends on what is common. In series the current is shared, so P ∝ R and the largest resistance is hottest. In parallel the voltage is shared, so P ∝ 1/R and the smallest resistance is hottest.

A bulb marked 100 W, 220 V has R = V2/P = 484 Ω. Put two unequal bulbs in series and the lower-wattage one glows brighter, because it has the greater resistance — the standard trick question.

Power is transmitted at high voltage precisely to keep I, and so the I2R loss in the cables, small.

Hold on to thisSeries → P ∝ R. Parallel → P ∝ 1/R. Work out which is common before choosing the formula.

2. Current Electricity

C8Concept
The galvanometer, and its two conversions

A moving coil galvanometer carries a coil in a radial field, so the torque is NIAB at every position and the deflection is proportional to the current:

NIAB = Cφ  ⇒  φ ∝ I
To an ammeter
a low resistance shunt in parallel: S = IgG/(I − Ig)
To a voltmeter
a high resistance in series: R = V/Ig − G

An ideal ammeter has zero resistance and goes in series; an ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance and goes in parallel. Each is designed so as not to disturb what it measures.

Hold on to thisAmmeter: low, parallel shunt. Voltmeter: high, series. And the denominator in the shunt formula is (I − Ig), not I.

Two cells of about 5 V and 10 V are to be compared accurately on a 400 cm potentiometer. Which is the right arrangement? 3. Magnetic Effect of Current and Magnetism

C1Concept
The Biot–Savart law

Every little piece of a current-carrying wire makes its own contribution to the field:

dB = µ04πI dl sin θr2,    µ04π = 1 × 10−7 T m A−1

An emf appears whenever the flux changes — by changing B, or the area, or the orientation. A steady flux, however large, induces nothing.

C1Concept
Displacement current

Ampere's law in its original form fails for a charging capacitor: two surfaces bounded by the same loop, one cutting the wire and one passing between the plates, give different enclosed currents. Maxwell repaired it by noticing that the changing electric field between the plates does what a current does:

Id = ε0 dφEdt,    ∮B·dl = µ0(Ic + Id)

In the gap the displacement current is exactly equal to the conduction current in the leads, so the total current is continuous round the circuit and the law becomes consistent.

The consequence is enormous: a changing E makes B, and a changing B makes E. The two can sustain each other and travel — which is what an electromagnetic wave is.

Hold on to thisA changing electric field is as good as a current for making a magnetic field. That is the whole origin of light.

The energy is shared equally between the electric and magnetic fields.

6. Optics I — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments

C1Concept
The sign convention, and the two formulae

Everything in this chapter follows from one convention, applied without exception: measure all distances from the pole or the optical centre, positive in the direction the light travels. Heights above the axis are positive.

Mirror: 1v + 1u = 1f = 2R    Lens: 1v − 1u = 1f
mmirror = −vu    mlens = vu

A real object gives u negative. Concave mirrors and convex lenses have f positive; convex mirrors and concave lenses negative.

Watch outThe two formulae differ by a sign, and so do the two magnifications. Write the convention down at the top of the answer before you substitute — more marks are lost here than to any concept.
Hold on to thisState the convention, then substitute with signs. A positive v for a mirror means a virtual image; for a lens it means a real one.

Refraction, and total internal reflection
n = sin isin r = cv = λvacuumλmedium

Going from denser to rarer the ray bends away from the normal, so beyond a certain angle it cannot get out at all:

sin C = nrarerndenser  (= 1/n for a medium against air)

Two conditions, both needed: the light must go from denser to rarer, and the angle of incidence must exceed C. Then the reflection is total — brighter than any mirror, since nothing is transmitted.

Diamond sparkles
n = 2·42, so C ≈ 24° and light is trapped through many reflections
Optical fibre
repeated total internal reflection down the core
Mirage, and the 45° prism
the same effect
Hold on to thisDenser to rarer, and i > C. Optical density is not mass density — turpentine is optically denser than water but lighter.

C3Concept
The prism: deviation, minimum deviation and dispersion
A + Δ = i + e,    A = r1 + r2

Plot Δ against i and it falls, passes through a single minimum, then rises. At the minimum the ray passes symmetrically: i = e and r1 = r2 = A/2, which gives

n = sin[(A+Δm)/2]sin(A/2)

For a thin prism Δ = (n−1)A, so different colours are deviated differently — dispersion. Violet has the larger n, so it is deviated most; red least.

Angular dispersion = (nV−nR)A,    ω = nV−nRnY−1
Hold on to thisAt minimum deviation everything is symmetric. And for every other deviation there are two angles of incidence that give it.

D1Derivation
Refraction at a single spherical surface, and the lens maker's formula

For a single surface of radius R separating media n1 and n2, small-angle geometry gives

n2v − n1u = n2 − n1R

A thin lens is two such surfaces. Apply the result at the first (radius R1), then use that image as the object for the second (radius R2), where the light returns to n1. Adding the two equations makes the intermediate image distance cancel.

Result1/f = (n−1)(1/R1 − 1/R2). Note n is the relative index: a glass lens in water is much weaker, and in a denser medium it changes sign altogether.

The microscope and the telescope
Compound microscope
M = (vo/uo)(1 + D/fe) at the near point; (vo/uo)(D/fe) at infinity. Both fo and fe small, fo the smaller.
Astronomical telescope
M = fo/fe in normal adjustment, with length fo+fe; (fo/fe)(1 + fe/D) at the near point. fo large and the aperture wide, fe small.

  • Distant object method — focus a far object on a screen; the lens-to-screen distance is f.

C1Concept
Huygens' principle and the shape of a wavefront

Every point of a wavefront acts as a fresh source of secondary wavelets, which travel forward at the wave speed; the envelope of those wavelets is the wavefront an instant later.

Point source
spherical wavefronts
Line source
cylindrical
Source at infinity
plane — which is why sunlight reaching the earth is treated as plane

The construction assumes nothing about the wave being transverse, so it applies to sound as readily as to light. A ray is simply the normal to the wavefront.

When light enters a denser medium the frequency is fixed by the source, so it is the speed and wavelength that fall.

Hold on to thisWavefront = surface of constant phase; ray = its normal. Point → spherical, infinity → plane.

7. Optics II — Wave Optics

D1Derivation
Reflection and refraction from Huygens' construction

Let the plane wavefront AB meet the surface at O, while B still has a time t to travel before it reaches O′.

Reflection. In that time the wavelet from O spreads a distance OC = ct in the same medium, and BO′ = ct too. The right-angled triangles OBO′ and OCO′ share OO′, have BO′ = OC, and have right angles at B and C, so they are congruent (RHS). Hence i = r.

Refraction. The same construction with the wavelet now spreading at the slower speed c2, so OC = c2t is shorter than BO′ = c1t. Then sin i/sin r = c1/c2 = n.

Resulti = r, and Snell's law — both from one construction, and both with the incident ray, normal and refracted ray in one plane.

7. Optics II — Wave Optics

C2Concept
Coherence, and the conditions for interference

Two sources are coherent if they emit the same frequency with a constant phase difference. That constancy is what makes a pattern stand still long enough to be seen.

  • Coherent sources are obtained by splitting a single wavefront — the two slits of Young's experiment, Fresnel's biprism, Lloyd's mirror.
  • Two independent lamps cannot be coherent: each changes phase randomly some 108 times a second, and the pattern washes out.
  • For good contrast the two amplitudes should be nearly equal, and the sources close together and narrow.
I ∝ (a1+a2)2 at a maximum,  (a1−a2)2 at a minimum

Interference redistributes energy; it never creates or destroys it. The average intensity over the pattern is exactly what the two sources would give separately.

Hold on to thisConstant phase difference, from one wavefront split in two. Energy is redistributed, not lost.

7. Optics II — Wave Optics

D2Derivation
Fringe width in Young's double slit experiment

With slits a distance d apart and a screen D away, take a point P a height y from the centre and drop AN perpendicular to BP.

Because D >> d the two rays are nearly parallel, so the path difference is

Δx = BN = d sin θ ≈ d tan θ = ydD

Bright fringes need Δx = nλ, so yn = nλD/d. The fringe width is the gap between consecutive ones.

Resultβ = λD/d, the same for bright and dark fringes, so the pattern is evenly spaced. The two approximations — nearly parallel rays, and small θ — are where the marks are.

7. Optics II — Wave Optics

C4Concept
Single-slit diffraction

A single slit of width a gives a broad central maximum flanked by much weaker ones. Minima, not maxima, are where the arithmetic is simple:

a sin θ = nλ (minima),    half-width θ = λa
angular width of the central maximum = 2λa,   linear width = 2λDa

Note the inversion: narrowing the slit widens the pattern. And the contrast with interference is the examinable point — interference gives evenly spaced fringes of equal brightness, diffraction one dominant central maximum with the rest falling away fast.

Hold on to thisMinima at a sin θ = nλ, central width 2λ/a. Narrower slit, wider pattern.
