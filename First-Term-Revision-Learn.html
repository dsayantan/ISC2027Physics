Electrostatics

Coulomb’s law and superposition; electric field and field lines; electric flux and Gauss’ theorem with its three standard applications; dipoles; potential, potential energy and equipotentials; capacitance, combinations of capacitors and dielectrics.

Type 1 · Superposition, and where the field or the force vanishes

How to attack itForces and fields are vectors — always resolve, and always exploit symmetry before you compute. For a ‘where is the force zero’ question, first decide which region the point must lie in: between two like charges, and outside the smaller of two unlike charges. Then set the two magnitudes equal and solve.
P13 marksNCERT Exemplar 1.23
Two charges q and −3q are fixed on the x-axis, separated by a distance d. Where should a third charge 2q be placed so that it experiences no force?
  1. The charges are unlike, so the two forces on 2q can only oppose each other outside the segment, and then only beyond the smaller charge q. Put 2q at a distance x from q, on the side away from −3q.
  2. Distances are then x to q and (d + x) to −3q. Equating magnitudes:
    2q·q4πε0 x2 = 2q·3q4πε0 (d+x)2
  3. So (d + x)2 = 3x2, i.e. 2x2 − 2dx − d2 = 0.
    x = 2d ± √4d2 + 8d24 = d(1 ± √3)2
  4. Only the positive root is physical.

Answer At x = d(1 + √3)/2 ≈ 1·37d from q, on the side away from −3q.

Trap Do not look for the point between them. Between two unlike charges both forces on a positive test charge point the same way, so they can never cancel there.

P23 marksNCERT Exemplar 1.25
Five charges, each q, are placed at the corners A, B, C, D, E of a regular pentagon of circumradius r, centre O. Find the field at O (i) as it stands, (ii) if the charge at A is removed, (iii) if the charge at A is replaced by −q. Does the answer change for a regular n-gon?
  1. (i) The five equal charges sit at the vertices of a regular polygon, so their five field vectors at O are equal in magnitude and equally spaced in direction. By symmetry they sum to zero.
  2. (ii) Removing q at A is the same as keeping all five and adding −q at A. The five give zero, so only the added charge is left:
    E = q4πε0 r2 , directed along OA
  3. (iii) Replacing q by −q is the same as adding −2q at A, so the field doubles:
    E = 2q4πε0 r2 , along OA

Answer (i) zero  (ii) q/4πε0r2 along OA  (iii) 2q/4πε0r2 along OA. For a regular n-gon the answers are exactly the same — only the regularity was used, never the number five.

Note This ‘add and subtract the missing charge’ trick turns a four-vector sum into a one-line answer. It is worth recognising whenever a symmetric arrangement has one item removed or flipped.

P33 marksISC 2026 specimen Q10(i)
Five identical charges Q = 2 µC are placed at equal intervals on a semicircle of radius 2 cm, and a charge q = 1 µC sits at the centre. Calculate the force on q.
  1. Each charge alone would give
    F0 = kqQR2 = (9 × 109)(1 × 10−6)(2 × 10−6)(0·02)2 = 45 N
  2. Take the horizontal diameter as the axis. The two end charges are diametrically opposite, so their forces cancel.
  3. The two at ±45° have their transverse components cancelling and their axial components adding:
    2F0 cos 45° = 2(45)(0·707) = 63·6 N
  4. The middle charge, on the axis, contributes 45 N the same way.

Answer F = 63·6 + 45 = 108·6 N, along the axis of symmetry, directed away from the middle charge.

Trap Adding all five magnitudes gives 225 N and no marks. The cancellation argument is the question.

P41 markHeritage First Term 2024–25, Q1(A)(vii)
Charges Q, Q and q sit at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side a. The electrostatic potential energy of the system is zero. Find q.
  1. Sum over the three pairs, all at the same separation a:
    U = ka (Q2 + Qq + Qq) = kQa (Q + 2q)
  2. Setting U = 0 gives Q + 2q = 0.

Answer q = −Q/2

Note With three identical charges the same sum gives U = 3kq2/a — the form asked in ISC 2020 Q1(A)(i) and 2025 Improvement Q4(i).

P51 markCISCE competency booklet, Q20
A charged sphere A (+q) on an insulated handle is brought a fixed distance above a non-conducting sphere B (+Q) resting on a spring, and the spring compresses by d1. B is then replaced by a similar conducting sphere and A is brought to the same distance without touching it; the compression is now d2. Compare d1 and d2.
  1. On the non-conducting sphere the charge cannot move, so the repulsion is that of two effectively fixed charge distributions.
  2. On the conducting sphere the free electrons are attracted towards A and move to the near side, leaving the far side more positive. The charge is no longer symmetrically placed: the negative charge sits closer to A and the positive charge further away.
  3. Since force falls as 1/r2, the attraction to the near negative charge is weighted more heavily, so the net repulsion is reduced and the spring compresses less.

Answer d2 < d1

Note Induction on a conductor always produces a net attractive contribution, which is why a charged rod attracts uncharged metal — and why it reduces a repulsion here.

Type 2 · Gauss’ theorem: counting the flux

How to attack itFlux depends on the net charge enclosed and on nothing else — not on the size or shape of the surface, not on where inside the charge sits, and not on charges outside. So these questions are always arithmetic on the enclosed charge, never an integral.
P61 markNCERT Exemplar 1.14
An arbitrary closed surface encloses an electric dipole. What is the electric flux through it?
  1. A dipole is +q and −q, so the net enclosed charge is zero.

Answer Zero.

Note The field on the surface is emphatically not zero — only its net outward flux is. Field lines that enter also leave.

P73 marksNCERT Exemplar 1.19
A charge q is placed at (a) a corner of a cube of side a, (b) the mid-point of one edge, (c) the centre of one face. What is the total flux through the faces of the cube in each case?
  1. Surround the charge by however many identical cubes are needed to enclose it completely, and share the flux q/ε0 equally between them.
  2. Corner. Eight cubes meet at a corner, so the cube gets one eighth.
    φ = q8ε0
  3. Mid-point of an edge. Four cubes meet along an edge:
    φ = q4ε0
  4. Centre of a face. Two cubes meet across a face:
    φ = q2ε0

Answer q/8ε0, q/4ε0 and q/2ε0 respectively.

Note The same counting gives q/6ε0 for one face of a cube with the charge at its centre — six faces sharing the whole flux.

P82 marksNCERT Exemplar 1.17
If the total charge enclosed by a surface is zero, does it follow that the electric field is zero everywhere on the surface? Conversely, if the field is zero everywhere on the surface, does it follow that the enclosed charge is zero?
  1. First direction: no. Zero net charge makes the flux zero, not the field. A surface enclosing a dipole has a perfectly good field on it; the inward and outward contributions merely cancel. The field could also be everywhere tangential to the surface, giving zero flux with a non-zero field.
  2. Converse: yes. If E = 0 at every point of the surface then the integral ∮E·dA vanishes, so by Gauss’ theorem the enclosed charge is zero.

Answer No for the first; yes for the converse.

Trap ‘Flux zero’ and ‘field zero’ are different statements. Only one of the two implications holds.

P92 marksISC 2026 board Q2(i)
Charges Q1 = +17·7 µC, Q2 = −8·85 µC and Q3 = −17·7 µC lie inside a closed surface S, while Q4 = 35·4 µC lies outside it. Calculate the flux emerging from S.
  1. Q4 is outside, so it contributes nothing.
    qenc = 17·7 − 8·85 − 17·7 = −8·85 µC
    φ = qencε0 = −8·85 × 10 × 10−68·85 × 10−12

Answer φ = −1 × 106 N m2 C−1, i.e. the flux is inward.

Note Companion one-markers: the flux through a sphere is unchanged if its radius is doubled, and becomes zero if the enclosed point charge is replaced by a dipole (ISC 2024 Q2(i)).

Type 3 · The three standard Gauss fields, and conductors

How to attack itThree results carry every numerical: line charge E = λ/2πε0r (falls as 1/r), sheet E = σ/2ε0 (does not fall at all), shell E = kq/r2 outside and zero inside. For a conductor the field just outside is σ/ε0, because all the flux leaves on one side.
P102 marksNCERT Exemplar 1.15
A metallic spherical shell has inner radius R1 and outer radius R2. A charge Q is placed at the centre of the cavity. Find the surface charge density on (i) the inner surface and (ii) the outer surface.
  1. The field inside the metal must be zero, so a Gaussian surface drawn within the metal encloses no net charge. The inner surface must therefore carry −Q, induced by Q at the centre.
  2. The shell was neutral overall, so the outer surface carries +Q.
  3. Dividing by the respective areas:

Answer (i) −Q4πR12   (ii) Q4πR22

Note This is why a charged object inside a closed metal box produces a field outside it, but a charge on the box produces none inside.

P112 marksNCERT Exemplar 1.16
The dimensions of an atom are of the order of an Ångström, so there must be very large electric fields between its protons and electrons. Why, then, is the electrostatic field inside a conductor zero?
  1. Those atomic fields bind electrons to nuclei and are confined within each neutral atom; on the scale of many atoms they average to zero.
  2. A macroscopic field inside a conductor could only be produced by excess charge, and any excess charge in an isolated conductor moves to the outer surface until the interior field is exactly cancelled — which is the electrostatic equilibrium condition.

Answer The atomic fields belong to neutral entities and cancel on average; excess charge, which is what could create a bulk field, resides entirely on the outer surface.

P123 marksISC 2025 board Q9
An infinite plane sheet carries surface charge density +σ. P is a point a small distance r to its right. (i) Write the field at P. (ii) An identical sheet of density −σ is now placed parallel to the first, with P between them. What is the resultant field at P, and its direction? (iii) What is the field outside the pair?
  1. (i) A Gaussian pill box through the sheet gives 2EA = σA/ε0:
    E = σ2ε0 , independent of r
  2. (ii) Between the sheets both fields run from the + sheet to the − sheet, so they add:
    E = σ2ε0 + σ2ε0 = σε0
  3. (iii) Outside the pair the two fields are equal and opposite, so they cancel.

Answer (i) σ/2ε0  (ii) σ/ε0, directed from the positive sheet to the negative sheet, normal to both  (iii) zero.

Trap For a conductor of surface density σ the field just outside is σ/ε0, not σ/2ε0 — the field inside is zero, so the whole flux leaves on one side. Note that ISC has used the thin-sheet formula even for a question worded ‘large conducting sheet’ (2023 specimen 1A(i)); write the formula you use and the method mark is safe.

Type 4 · Dipoles — field, torque and energy

How to attack itLearn the two field results and the 2 : 1 ratio between them: axial E = 2kp/r3, equatorial E = kp/r3. In a uniform field a dipole feels a couple but no net force; in a non-uniform field it also feels a net force towards the stronger region.
P132 marksISC 2025 board Q3 · Heritage 2024–25 Q1(B)(vii)
For a short electric dipole: (i) what is the locus of the points at which the potential is zero? (ii) If E1 is the field at a point on the axis and E2 that at an equidistant point on the equatorial line, find E1/E2.
  1. (i) Every point of the equatorial plane is equidistant from +q and −q. Potential is a scalar, so the two contributions are equal and opposite and add to zero.
  2. (ii) For a short dipole
    Eaxial = 2kpr3 ,   Eequat = kpr3

Answer (i) the equatorial plane  (ii) 2 : 1

Note Hence the work done in moving a charge along the perpendicular bisector is zero — ISC 2026 Q1(B)(iii) asks exactly that.

P141 markHeritage First Term 2024–25, Q1(A)(vi)
A dipole of charges ±2 mC separated by 4 cm is placed at 30° to a field of 105 N C−1. Find the torque on it.
  1. p = q × 2l = (2 × 10−3)(0·04) = 8 × 10−5 C m
    τ = pE sin θ = (8 × 10−5)(105)(0·5)

Answer τ = 4 N m

Trap 4 cm is the whole separation 2l, not l. And the m in mC is milli, not micro.

P151 markNCERT Exemplar 1.5
A dipole is placed in a field whose lines are closer together on the left than on the right, with the dipole’s −q end on the left and +q end on the right. Which way does it experience a force?
  1. Field lines crowded on the left means the field is stronger on the left.
  2. The −q end sits in the stronger field and is pulled left (against the field); the +q end sits in the weaker field and is pushed right. The larger of the two forces acts on −q.

Answer Towards the left, i.e. towards the stronger field.

Note A dipole in a uniform field feels no net force at all. Whenever a question gives you a net force on a dipole, the field must be non-uniform.

P162 marksISC 2025 & 2026 specimen Q3
In a water molecule the two O—H bonds each have length l, the angle between them is θ, and each hydrogen carries charge e. Find the dipole moment of the molecule.
  1. Each bond is a dipole of moment el, and the two are at θ to each other.
  2. Two equal vectors at θ add along the bisector, with magnitude 2p cos(θ/2):

Answer p = 2el cos(θ/2), along the bisector of the H—O—H angle, pointing from the oxygen towards the hydrogens.

Type 5 · Potential, potential energy and equipotentials

How to attack itV and U are scalars: add them algebraically, with signs, and never resolve components. An equipotential surface is everywhere perpendicular to the field, so no work is done moving along one; equipotentials crowd where the field is strong.
P171 markNCERT Exemplar 2.4
The potential on the surface of a charged conducting sphere is 100 V. S1: the field at every interior point is zero. S2: the potential at every interior point is 100 V. Which is correct? (a) S1 true, S2 false  (b) both false  (c) both true, and S1 is the cause of S2  (d) both true but independent.
  1. Inside a conductor in equilibrium E = 0, so S1 is true.
  2. Since E = −dV/dr, a zero field means V cannot change: the interior is all at the surface value, 100 V. So S2 is true, and it follows from S1.

Answer (c)

Trap The two statements are not independent, which rules out (d). A constant potential and a zero field are the same fact stated twice.

P182 marksNCERT Exemplar 2.8 (multiple correct)
Which of the following are true of equipotential surfaces? (a) they are closer together where the field is large; (b) they are more crowded near a sharp edge of a conductor; (c) they are more crowded near regions of large charge density; (d) they are always equally spaced.
  1. E = −dV/dr, so for a fixed step in V the spacing dr is small exactly where E is large. (a) is true.
  2. Charge collects at sharp points, so the field — and therefore the crowding — is greatest there. (b) and (c) are true.
  3. (d) would require a uniform field everywhere, which is not general.

Answer (a), (b) and (c).

Note Multiple-correct items are an Exemplar speciality and the ISC papers never set them. They are worth doing precisely because they force you to check every option instead of stopping at the first plausible one.

P192 marksNCERT Exemplar 2.17 · 2.18
(i) Can the potential function have a maximum or a minimum in free space? (ii) A test charge is carried round two different closed paths in the field of a point charge, one path following field lines and normals and the other a rectangle of the same area. Compare the work done.
  1. (i) No. A maximum or minimum of V would be a point of stable equilibrium for a charge, and would require field lines to converge on or diverge from a point in empty space. Field lines begin and end only on charges, so in charge-free space V has neither.
  2. (ii) The electrostatic field is conservative, so the work round any closed path is zero, whatever its shape or area.

Answer (i) No.  (ii) Zero in both cases, and therefore equal.

Note This is Earnshaw’s theorem in disguise: you cannot trap a charge in a purely electrostatic field.

P202 marksNCERT Exemplar 2.14 · 2.15
(i) Two conducting spheres of radii R1 > R2 are at the same potential. Which has the greater surface charge density? (ii) Do free electrons drift towards higher or lower potential?
  1. (i) At the same potential V = kQ/R, so Q ∝ R. Then
    σ = Q4πR2 ∝ RR2 , so σ ∝ 1R
  2. So the smaller sphere has the larger charge density.
  3. (ii) Electrons carry negative charge, so the force on them is opposite to E. They therefore drift from low potential to high potential — opposite to the conventional current.

Answer (i) the smaller sphere  (ii) towards higher potential

Note σ ∝ 1/R is why charge concentrates at sharp points, and why a lightning conductor is spiked.

P212 marksNCERT textbook, Ch 2 Exercise 2.2
A regular hexagon of side 10 cm has a charge of 5 µC at each of its six vertices. Calculate the potential at the centre.
  1. For a regular hexagon the circumradius equals the side, so every vertex is 0·10 m from the centre.
  2. Potential is a scalar, so the six contributions simply add — no resolution of components, and no cancellation.
    V = 6 × kqr = 6 × (9 × 109)(5 × 10−6)0·10
    = 6 × 4·5 × 105 = 2·7 × 106 V

Answer V = 2·7 × 106 V = 2·7 MV

Trap Contrast the field at the same point, which is zero by symmetry. Six equal vectors at 60° cancel; six equal scalars do not.

Type 6 · Capacitors, dielectrics and stored energy

How to attack itSeries carries the same charge; parallel shares the same voltage. Before touching a dielectric question, decide whether the battery is still connected (V fixed) or has been disconnected (Q fixed) — that single choice decides every other answer.
P223 marksNCERT Exemplar 2.20
A capacitor with a dielectric between its plates is charged from a d.c. source. The battery is then disconnected and the dielectric is removed. State whether the capacitance, the stored energy, the field, the charge and the voltage increase, decrease or stay the same.
  1. The battery is disconnected, so the charge is trapped: Q is constant. That is the fact everything else follows from.
  2. Removing the dielectric removes the factor K: C = Kε0A/d falls to ε0A/d, so C decreases.
  3. V = Q/C with Q fixed and C smaller, so V increases; and E = V/d, so E increases.
  4. U = Q2/2C with Q fixed and C smaller, so U increases.

Answer C decreases; Q unchanged; V increases; E increases; U increases.

Trap Using U = ½CV2 here invites the wrong answer, because V is not the fixed quantity. Pick the energy formula written in terms of whatever is being held constant: Q2/2C when Q is fixed, ½CV2 when V is fixed. The extra energy comes from the work you do pulling the slab out.

P233 marksISC 2020 board Q14
A parallel plate capacitor is charged by a battery, which is then disconnected. A dielectric of constant K is now introduced, filling the space completely. State, in terms of K, the effect on (i) the capacitance, (ii) the potential difference, (iii) the stored energy.
  1. Again Q is fixed. Inserting the slab multiplies C by K.
  2. V = Q/C, so V falls to V/K.
  3. U = Q2/2C, so U falls to U/K.

Answer (i) KC  (ii) V/K  (iii) U/K

Note Contrast the case with the battery still connected: then V is fixed, Q rises to KQ and U rises to KU. Exactly the opposite conclusion from the same slab.

P242 marksISC 2023 specimen Q2(i)
You have many identical 100 µF capacitors. How would you connect the minimum number of them to obtain 75 µF?
  1. 75 = 100 × ¾, and a series step produces fractions, so aim for 1/C = 1/100 + 1/300.
  2. Put three in parallel: Cp = 300 µF.
  3. Put that group in series with a fourth:
    1C = 1100 + 1300 = 4300 , so C = 75 µF

Answer Four capacitors — three in parallel, that group in series with the fourth.

P253 marksNCERT Exemplar 2.29
Two metal spheres of radii R and 2R carry the same surface charge density σ. They are brought into contact and separated again. Find the new surface charge densities.
  1. Initial charges: Q1 = σ·4πR2 and Q2 = σ·4π(2R)2 = 4Q1, so the total is 5Q1.
  2. On contact they reach a common potential, so kQ′1/R = kQ′2/2R, giving Q′2 = 2Q′1.
  3. Charge is conserved: 3Q′1 = 5Q1, so Q′1 = 5Q1/3 and Q′2 = 10Q1/3.
  4. Dividing by the areas 4πR2 and 16πR2:

Answer σ′1 = 5σ/3 on the small sphere and σ′2 = 5σ/6 on the large one — a ratio of 2 : 1, the inverse of the radii.

Note Charge shares to equalise potential, not density. The density ends up larger on the smaller sphere, consistent with σ ∝ 1/R in Type 5.

Type 1 · Drift velocity, resistivity and temperature

How to attack itEverything hangs off two relations: I = neAvd and ρ = m/ne2τ. Ohm’s law holds because τ barely depends on the applied field; resistivity changes with temperature because τ (and, in a semiconductor, n) does.
P261 markNCERT Exemplar 3.6
Which characteristic of the electrons determines the current in a conductor? (a) drift velocity alone  (b) thermal velocity alone  (c) both  (d) neither.
  1. Thermal velocities are large (~105 m s−1) but randomly directed, so they carry no net charge in any direction and contribute nothing to the current.
  2. Only the small ordered drift velocity (~10−4 m s−1) survives the averaging, and I = neAvd.

Answer (a) drift velocity alone.

P271 markNCERT Exemplar 3.5
A metal rod of length 10 cm and cross-section 1 cm × ½ cm is connected to a battery across opposite faces. The resistance is maximum when the battery is connected across which pair of faces?
  1. R = ρl/A, so R is largest when the current path is longest and the area it crosses smallest.
  2. The longest path is 10 cm, and the faces perpendicular to it are the 1 cm × ½ cm faces — the smallest pair.

Answer Across the 1 cm × ½ cm faces.

Trap Resistance is a property of the conductor and how it is connected, not of the material alone. The same rod has three different resistances.

P283 marksNCERT Exemplar 3.9 (multiple correct) · 3.13
The temperature dependence of ρ(T) for metals, semiconductors and insulators rests on which factors? (a) the number of carriers can change with T; (b) the interval between collisions can depend on T; (c) the length of the material can depend on T; (d) the carrier mass can depend on T. Explain also why τ is nearly independent of the applied field but strongly dependent on temperature.
  1. From ρ = m/ne2τ only n and τ are candidates, so (a) and (b) are the factors. Thermal expansion (c) is a utterly negligible effect on ρ, and the mass (d) is a constant.
  2. Metals: n is fixed, τ falls as the lattice vibrates more, so ρ rises.
  3. Semiconductors: n rises steeply as bonds break, far outpacing the fall in τ, so ρ falls.
  4. τ depends on the electrons’ speeds. The applied field changes those speeds by only about a millimetre per second — utterly negligible against thermal speeds — which is why Ohm’s law holds. A change in temperature changes them by ~102 m s−1, which is why ρ depends on T.

Answer (a) and (b).

Note This is the cleanest statement of why Ohm’s law is a good law at all: it is not exact, it is a consequence of drift speeds being tiny compared with thermal speeds.

P292 marksNCERT Exemplar 3.26
Two conductors of the same material and length: A is a solid wire of diameter 1 mm; B is a hollow tube of outer diameter 2 mm and inner diameter 1 mm. Find RA : RB.
  1. Same ρ and same l, so R ∝ 1/A.
    AA = π(0·5)2 = 0·25π ,   AB = π(12 − 0·52) = 0·75π (mm2)
    RARB = ABAA = 0·750·25 = 3

Trap Subtract the areas, not the diameters. The hollow tube has three times the cross-section of the solid wire despite looking thinner.

Type 2 · Cells, internal resistance and combinations

How to attack itV = ε − Ir is the whole subject. Series adds emfs and internal resistances; parallel keeps the emf and divides the internal resistance. For a mixed grouping of n cells per row and m rows, εeq = nε and req = nr/m.
P311 markNCERT Exemplar 3.2
Two batteries of emf ε1 and ε2 (ε2 > ε1) with internal resistances r1 and r2 are connected in parallel. Which is true? (a) ε1 < εeq < ε2  (b) εeq < ε1  (c) εeq = ε1 + ε2 always  (d) εeq is independent of r1, r2.
  1. For two cells in parallel
    εeq = ε1r2 + ε2r1r1 + r2
  2. This is a weighted average of ε1 and ε2, so it must lie between them. It plainly does depend on the internal resistances, which kills (d), and adding emfs is the series rule, which kills (c).

Answer (a)

Note Sanity check: equal internal resistances give the plain mean (ε1+ε2)/2, which is between them.

P322 marksNCERT Exemplar 3.24

How to attack itThree families, told apart by the sign and size of χ: diamagnetic (χ small and negative, temperature independent), paramagnetic (χ small and positive, χ ∝ 1/T by Curie’s law), ferromagnetic (χ huge, becomes paramagnetic above the Curie temperature).

Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Current

Type 1 · Lenz’s law — getting the direction right

How to attack itAsk three questions in order: is the flux changing? If nothing moves and the field is steady, there is no emf at all. If it is changing, is it increasing or decreasing? Then the induced effect always opposes that change — which fixes the direction, the sign, and often the mechanical force too.
P762 marksNCERT Exemplar 6.11
A stationary bar magnet is surrounded by a closed wire loop with a switch. The switch is closed. Does a current flow?
  1. No part of the wire moves, so there is no motional emf.
  2. The magnet is stationary, so the field — and hence the flux through the loop — does not change with time.
  3. Faraday’s law needs a rate of change of flux. A large flux with zero rate of change induces nothing.

Answer No current flows.

Trap A big flux is not an emf. Only dφ/dt matters, which is why closing the switch on a static arrangement does nothing.

P773 marksNCERT Exemplar 6.12 · 6.13
A tightly wound solenoid carries a steady current from a d.c. source. State, with reasons, what happens to the current when (i) the turns of the solenoid are pulled slightly apart, (ii) an iron core is inserted into it.
  1. (i) Pulling the turns apart lets flux leak out through the gaps, so the flux linkage falls. By Lenz’s law the induced emf opposes that fall, which it can only do by increasing the current.
  2. (ii) Inserting iron multiplies the field by μr, so the flux linkage rises sharply. The induced emf opposes the rise, so the current decreases.

Answer (i) the current increases; (ii) the current decreases.

Note Both are transient: once the change stops, the current settles back to ε/R, which the inductance cannot affect. Lenz’s law governs the response to change, not the steady state.

P783 marksNCERT Exemplar 6.14 · 6.15
A metal ring rests on top of a solenoid, supported by a card. (i) The solenoid current is switched on — what happens to the ring? (ii) Starting from a steady current, the current is now made to decrease — what happens?
  1. (i) Initially no flux threads the ring; switching on makes it increase. Lenz’s law says the ring opposes the increase, so the induced current circulates opposite to the solenoid current. Antiparallel currents repel, so the ring jumps up.
  2. (ii) Now the flux is decreasing, so the ring’s induced current flows in the same sense as the solenoid’s. Parallel currents attract, so the ring is pressed down — it stays on the card, and the card’s upward reaction increases.

Answer (i) it flies upward; (ii) it is pushed down onto the card.

Note This is the classic jumping-ring demonstration. The direction reverses purely because the sign of dφ/dt reverses.

P792 marksNCERT Exemplar 6.16
A bar magnet takes longer to fall through a metal pipe than a similar unmagnetised iron bar does. Explain.
  1. As the magnet falls, the flux through each ring-shaped element of the pipe changes, so eddy currents circulate in the pipe wall.
  2. By Lenz’s law those currents oppose the change, which means they exert a retarding force on the falling magnet — a magnetic braking force that grows with speed.
  3. The unmagnetised bar produces no such changing flux, so it falls freely under gravity.

Answer Eddy currents induced in the pipe oppose the magnet’s motion and retard its fall; the unmagnetised bar induces none.

Note The same effect is electromagnetic braking in trains, and the reason a transformer core must be laminated.

How to attack itThe essential condition is a constant phase difference — coherence. Two independent sources can never be coherent, which is why a single wavefront is always split. Bright when the path difference is nλ, dark when it is (n+½)λ.

  2. Red and blue light have different frequencies, so their phase difference at any point varies with time at the difference frequency — there is no steady superposition at all.

Trap This is the sharpest test of whether you have understood coherence. Two beams of different colours cannot interfere, however carefully they are aligned.

    Δx = BP − AP = BN = d sin θ

Note The dark fringes give the same spacing, so bright and dark fringes are equally wide and equally spaced. The two approximations — nearly parallel rays, and small θ — are where the marks are.

Slits 5 mm apart are lit by 600 nm light with the screen 1·2 m away. Find the distance between the 5th bright fringe on one side and the 3rd on the other side. What changes at 500 nm?
  1. β = λDd = (600 × 10−9)(1·2)5 × 10−3 = 1·44 × 10−4 m = 0·144 mm
  2. Opposite sides of the centre, so the distances add:
    5β + 3β = 8β = 8(0·144) = 1·152 mm

P1431 markNCERT Exemplar 10.8
For the diffraction pattern of a small pinhole, as the hole is made larger: (a) the size decreases  (b) the intensity increases  (c) the size increases  (d) the intensity decreases.
  1. The angular width goes as λ/a, so a larger hole gives a smaller pattern — (a) true, (c) false.
  2. A larger hole admits more light, and it is concentrated into a smaller patch, so the intensity increases — (b) true, (d) false.

Answer (a) and (b).

Note This is why diffraction is invisible in daily life: everyday apertures are enormous compared with 500 nm, so the pattern shrinks to nothing and geometrical optics takes over.

P1442 marksNCERT Exemplar 10.13
Why is the diffraction of sound waves so much more evident in daily life than the diffraction of light?
  1. Diffraction is appreciable only when the aperture is comparable with the wavelength.
  2. Audible sound spans 20 Hz to 20 kHz, i.e. wavelengths from about 15 m down to 15 mm — the size of doors, windows and furniture. So sound bends round them readily.
  3. Light has λ ≈ 10−7 m, so an aperture would have to be sub-micron before the effect showed.

Answer Because sound wavelengths are of everyday size while light wavelengths are hundreds of nanometres.
